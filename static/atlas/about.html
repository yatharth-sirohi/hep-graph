HEP Atlas is a map of experimental particle and astroparticle physics: facilities, accelerators, detectors, collaborations, people and results, drawn from sources an agent fetched and read, not from memory. This page explains what it covers, how it decided what to include, what its confidence words mean, and where the coverage is thin — with the numbers below read live from the data, not typed by hand.

What this is, and is not

The atlas is partial by design and says so. It covers the programmes a particle physicist would recognise as their own field — see the scope boundary below — but it does not claim to hold every experiment in the field's history, and most of what it does hold is researched to a "mandatory bar," not exhaustively.

Every record carries a coverage tier, which states how deeply the atlas intends to cover it — a plan, not an achievement — and a researched state (none/partial/full), which is the achievement:

Coverage tiers (docs/HEP_ATLAS_RESEARCH_METHOD.md §11)
TierMeaningA tier-complete record contains
A — FlagshipA programme a newcomer would expect to find and explore in depthFull history from proposal to shutdown/upgrade, every detector with technology and key specs, sited coordinates, a dated institution roster and leadership, major results with a verbatim quote, design reports and detector papers, an INSPIRE experiment id.
B — MajorScientifically important and well documented, but not a landing pageIntervals to at least construction/active/completion granularity, the principal detector's main specs, correct sites, a roster snapshot, two or three defining results, a TDR or detector paper.
C — CoveredAppears correctly on the globe and in search, with a real summaryIdentity, kind, host facility and site with coordinates, a lifecycle interval, a sourced plain-language summary, topic links, at least one result or document. A roster may be absent.
D — ListedPresent so the map is not misleading by omissionIdentity, host facility, an approximate lifecycle interval, topic links, and a record-level source. Explicitly incomplete.

Tier is not a claim of importance — a historically decisive but sparsely documented programme can sit at tier C, and a well-documented small experiment can sit at tier B. It answers "how much of this do we intend to hold?", not "how good was it?" The live entity and tier counts are in the Coverage section below.

Research method and source hierarchy

The atlas prefers the party closest to the fact, and prefers a party that has to live with being wrong: a collaboration is the authority on its own detector; a laboratory is the authority on its own accelerators; a journal article is the authority on a measurement; nobody is the authority on somebody else's history. Between two sources that disagree, the atlas takes the one that is primary rather than reporting, official rather than third-party, technical rather than promotional, and — when the fact can change — newer rather than older.

Every source is typed, and its rank (how close it sits to the fact) follows from the type:

  1. The collaboration's own web pages.
  2. The host laboratory's own web pages.
  3. The CERN Greybook, or an INSPIRE experiment record.
  4. A design report — CDR, TDR, technical proposal, letter of intent.
  5. The instrument paper for a detector.
  6. HEPData — the numbers behind a published figure or table.
  7. A PDG Review of Particle Physics chapter.
  8. A member institution's own group page, or a geocoder (ROR, GeoNames, OSM/Nominatim).
  9. A collaboration's public result page, a press release, or a funding-agency/strategy document.
  10. A refereed journal article, a preprint, or an INSPIRE literature record.
  11. Wikipedia, Wikidata, or anything else — news, blogs, talks, theses — used only as a pointer to a real source, never as the sole source of a critical value.

Rank is not a quality score for the claim, it is a distance-to-the-fact score: a PDG chapter outranks a collaboration's own page for a world-average number, because averaging is what PDG is for. A value marked verified must cite at least one source in the top ten ranks; a coordinate for a detector or underground-laboratory site may never rest solely on a geocoder or on a rank at or below institution-page level.

Scope. An entity is in scope if it is an accelerator-based particle-physics programme, or if it materially participates in a question the field itself would take to Snowmass, the European Strategy, or a P5-style prioritisation — the 2023 P5 report's three themes and six science drivers. Optical/radio astronomy, most nuclear-structure physics, applied and medical accelerators, and pure theory are out; borderline admissions (CTAO, gravitational-wave observatories as multimessenger partners, cosmological surveys referenced from topics) carry a note saying why.

Claims and evidence

A field is either sourced — a non-empty list of sources, and for coordinates, key specifications, baselines, a result's date/significance/type, an official-class membership row, a collaboration's formation date, or a day/month-precision interval bound, a locator as well ("p. 12, Table 1.1", "§4.2") — or it is null with a note saying what was looked at and what it did not say. "Unknown is a value": a plausible number typed from memory is never an acceptable substitute for a sourced null.

A recorded result carries a required claim: a verbatim quote, up to 300 characters, taken only from the publication's own title or abstract, or from the collaboration's own result page — never from a press release, PDG, or Wikipedia. The wording is checked mechanically: a result typed as a discovery requires "discover" in the quote or a cited source's title; observation requires "observ"; evidence requires "evidence" — so the badge on screen cannot say more than the collaboration itself said.

What "verified", "approximate", "uncertain" and "derived" mean

Every sourced value carries one of four confidence words. A note is required whenever it is not verified:

WordWhat it assertsWhen it is used
verifiedA source in the top ten ranks states this value directlyThe normal case — no badge is shown for it
derivedThe atlas computed it from sourced inputsUnit conversion, a great-circle distance from two sourced coordinates, a total from sourced parts
approximateThe source itself gives it approximately, or as a round number"about 1300 km", "~40,000 PMTs"
uncertainA source exists but is weak, ambiguous, or contradictedTwo sources disagree and neither is clearly better; the only source is a low-rank pointer; the wording is ambiguous

uncertain still requires a source — it describes a weak source, not a guess. A verified value whose only source later goes dead is not silently downgraded: the link checker flags it "review required" and a human decides, rather than the atlas quietly changing what it claims to know.

The timeline, in plain words

Nothing on the timeline is asserted directly for a given year — it is derived from the sourced start/end intervals on each record, the same way every time the page is loaded. Scrubbing to a year selects whichever sourced interval covers it; if two intervals could apply, the atlas prefers the one that is definitely happening (construction, active, shutdown, upgrade, or a terminal event) over one that is merely proposed, approved or planned, and among those, the one that started most recently. A year a record's intervals do not reach at all reads as unrecorded, in grey — that is a gap in the sources, not a claim that nothing was happening.

An interval whose evidence supports it exactly renders solid; one whose evidence is looser (an imprecise date at the edge of the selected range) renders hatched, for "possibly covers this year, not certainly." An open-ended interval (still running, no recorded end) extends only to the atlas's own build date — not into the future — and any year after that renders dimmed, labelled "assumed ongoing": a record's silence about tomorrow is not evidence that a programme is still active tomorrow.

Approximate locations

Every detector site should carry a real position for its detector hall or underground cavern. When no fetched source gives that exact position, the honest record is a campus-grade point instead — the laboratory's or site's general location — carrying a note saying what specifically is missing. Relabelling a campus-grade point as a precise detector position to satisfy validation is forbidden: what the coordinate is and how well it is known are recorded separately.

The globe draws these markers with a dashed outline and the panel says "Approximate location." The exact current count of sites at campus-grade precision — computed from the data, not fixed here — is in the Coverage section below.

Collaboration rosters

Institution membership is deliberately partial. Only a handful of collaborations currently have a full, dated, geographic roster of member institutions built into the atlas — the rest carry only whatever summary numbers (institution count, country count, member count) the collaboration's own page states, if it states them at all. The Coverage section below reads the live count of which collaborations have a built roster and which experiments still lack one.

Where a roster exists, each row's evidence class controls both what "member" means and its exact wording in the interface:

  • On the collaboration's own member list — the collaboration's own roster page states it.
  • On the official institution list — the official institution list states it, as of a given date.
  • INSPIRE author profile lists this collaboration — an individual's own INSPIRE record names the affiliation.
  • Listed with this affiliation on a paper (year); current membership not confirmed — seen on a recent publication, not independently reconfirmed.
  • Listed on a paper in (year); may no longer be a member — the same, but old enough that membership may have lapsed.
  • The institution's own page states participation — the institution, not the collaboration, is the source.
  • Self-reported — a personal page or CV.
  • Inferred, not verified — never used for a person; a weak, unconfirmed institutional inference only.

How results are worded

A result is recorded only when the collaboration itself presented it as one — a publication, or a conference note, not a rumour or an unpublished plot — and only when it is a first observation or discovery, evidence for a process, a best-or-first-of-its-kind measurement, a frontier-defining limit, a documented anomaly, a first detection, or a technology milestone. Routine updates to an existing measurement are not new results; they update the existing record.

The wording always follows the collaboration's own vocabulary, without inflating it — discovery > observation > evidence > measurement/precision measurement > limit > anomaly. If a collaboration itself called something "evidence," the atlas records it as evidence, regardless of what significance is quoted elsewhere — see Claims and evidence for the mechanical quote check behind that rule.

Local-map geometry

Some facilities also carry a to-scale local map — accelerator rings on their true paths, beamlines as lines, detector halls and experimental areas as footprints — in addition to the single point shown on the globe. The full contract for this layer, including what the validator enforces and how a campus is researched, is docs/HEP_ATLAS_GEOMETRY.md in the project repository.

Every drawn shape carries a representation class stating how it was obtained:

  • surveyed — coordinates published by the laboratory itself, or in a technical drawing.
  • georeferenced — traced from imagery or GIS data (OpenStreetMap, a national mapping agency) or an official plan aligned against surveyed control points.
  • derived — computed from sourced parameters and sourced anchor points, with the computation itself stated.
  • inferred direction — for a long-baseline neutrino beam, the bearing to a far site rather than a surveyed path; drawn and labelled explicitly as inferred, never as a real trajectory.

Where public evidence cannot support a shape, the facility stays visible in an "unlocated / geometry not verified" list with a sourced explanation — it is never drawn from a guess. Official diagrams that are not to scale (schematics) are offered as links only, labelled "not to scale," and are never presented as a geometry record.

Where OpenStreetMap data contributes to a shape or to the basemap tiles, the map carries the attribution © OpenStreetMap contributors under the Open Database License (ODbL) 1.0, as OpenStreetMap's own licence requires.

Report an error

Every record traces to a fetched source, but mistakes happen — a misread quote, a stale status, a coordinate that should be marked approximate and is not. If you find one, e-mail yatharth-sirohi@uiowa.edu naming the record and what looks wrong (the page's "Copy share link" gives a link to exactly what you are looking at); a link to the source that contradicts it is the fastest way to get it fixed.
